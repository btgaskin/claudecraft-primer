# Diagnostic methods

Adapted from the [Tradecraft Primer](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf), printed pages 7–15. Page numbers refer to the document's printed pagination.

## Key Assumptions Check — pp. 7–9

Use when framing an assessment or checking a conclusion that depends on uncertain premises.

1. State the current judgment.
2. Identify the stated and unstated premises needed to sustain it.
3. For each consequential premise, record its basis, conditions of validity, and what would undermine it.
4. Explain how the judgment changes if the premise fails. Reclassify an unsupported premise as an uncertainty to investigate.

Optional record: assumption; basis; consequence if wrong; revision condition. Prioritize assumptions that change the answer.

## Quality of Information Check — pp. 10–11

Use throughout a project, especially when a small number of reports carry the conclusion.

1. Trace decisive claims to their earliest accessible source. Record dates and how the information was obtained.
2. Assess the source's opportunity to know, the method's limitations, and the credibility of the particular claim separately.
3. Group reports with shared origins. Look for genuinely independent corroboration and relevant contradictions.
4. Check whether corrections, retractions, processing errors, or changed conditions affect the interpretation.
5. Identify the information gaps that most constrain the assessment and the next useful check.

Optional record: claim; source and locator; date; method/access; dependence; limitation; bearing on conclusion. Keep reported facts distinct from direct observations.

## Indicators or Signposts of Change — pp. 12–13

Use when an assessment could be revised by observable developments.

1. Identify the explanations or scenarios being tracked.
2. Specify observations that would strengthen or weaken each, including their expected timeframe.
3. Explain where the observations could be obtained and whether an apparent absence would be detectable.
4. Reassess the judgment as evidence arrives, considering reliability, dependence, and diagnostic value.

Optional record: observable; expected direction under each explanation; source; timeframe; implication. Set a review cadence only when ongoing monitoring is requested. Unlike the primer's simple counting suggestion, do not select a scenario by the number of changed indicators alone.

## Analysis of Competing Hypotheses — pp. 14–15

Use when several plausible explanations fit some of the available information.

1. Define the question and a useful set of alternatives. Include mixed causes or an unresolved alternative when warranted; state when the set is incomplete or overlapping.
2. Gather the evidence relevant to the alternatives, including material conflicts and source limitations.
3. Compare how expected each observation would be under each explanation. Apply the same relevant evidence to every alternative.
4. Identify the most diagnostic observations and the assumptions needed to interpret them. Explain consequential judgments in words.
5. Test sensitivity to unreliable evidence, dependencies, and missing observations.
6. Give the current assessment, viable alternatives, and the next observations that would discriminate among them.

Choose a table orientation that makes the comparison readable. For a compact summary, use one row per explanation with columns for supporting evidence, conflicting evidence, assumptions, and discriminating observations. A large matrix is optional.

This adaptation uses ACH as a comparison aid. It omits automatic inconsistency scoring and gives supporting evidence and relevant prior information substantive consideration. See [Evidence and adaptation](evidence.md) for research on ACH and information presentation.
