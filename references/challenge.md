# Challenge methods

Adapted from the [Tradecraft Primer](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf), printed pages 17–25.

## Devil's Advocacy — pp. 17–18

Use to test a dominant judgment, especially one resting on a consequential assumption.

- State the prevailing assessment fairly, including its strongest evidence.
- Identify its most vulnerable premise or interpretation.
- Develop the strongest plausible alternative using the available evidence. Identify evidence that would challenge the alternative too.
- Explain whether the exercise changes the conclusion, narrows its conditions, or reveals a useful research question.

Label the exercise so the reader can distinguish the proposed challenge from the final assessment. Strength of advocacy does not determine evidential strength.

## Team A/Team B — pp. 19–21

Use when there are developed competing views worth examining in comparable form.

- Give each position the same focal question and relevant evidence base.
- Present each position's claims, assumptions, strongest evidence, and weaknesses.
- Compare rebuttals and locate the factual, interpretive, or value disagreement that drives the difference.
- Identify evidence that could resolve it, and retain unresolved disagreement in the assessment.

For a single assistant, present this as a comparison of constructed positions. Do not describe simulated roles as independent experts or a peer jury. The technique does not itself authorize additional agents or external services.

## High-Impact/Low-Probability Analysis — pp. 22–23

Use when an apparently unlikely outcome has consequences important enough to examine.

- Define the outcome and why its consequences matter.
- Identify plausible pathways, dependencies, and possible triggers.
- Examine consequences, observable warning signs, and factors that would interrupt or enable the pathway.
- Keep the estimated likelihood and its basis separate from the case for preparedness.

An imaginable chain of events is a hypothesis to examine. Assess each consequential link and identify unsupported transitions. Apply the method to opportunities as well as adverse outcomes.

## What If Analysis — pp. 24–25

Use to investigate how a consequential outcome could occur, including one the current assessment discounts.

- State the current assessment and explicitly assume the alternative outcome for the exercise.
- Work backward through what would need to happen, then check the pathway forward for coherence.
- Separate established conditions from hypothetical triggers and causal links.
- Identify observable developments, obstacles, and consequences.
- Return to the original question and explain what the exercise changes about the assessment or decision.

This exercise emphasizes pathways; high-impact/low-probability analysis emphasizes consequences and preparedness. Neither establishes probability merely by telling a coherent story.
