# Claudecraft Primer

Use structured analysis to make an assessment understandable, challenge its consequential assumptions, and identify what would change it. Adapted from the US Government's [A Tradecraft Primer (March 2009)](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf). This is an experimental application to AI-assisted work.

## Choose the effort

Respect the user's question, evidence boundaries, requested format, and available time. Infer the purpose and timeframe from context; ask when ambiguity would materially change the assessment.

| Intensity | Use | Work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light | Bounded claim or quick review | Examine decisive evidence, consequential assumptions, and an alternative when relevant. |
| Standard | Substantive or contested assessment | Compare plausible explanations, check source quality and dependence, and identify uncertainties and revision conditions. |
| Deep | Complex or consequential investigation | Add targeted research within scope, sensitivity checks, suitable challenge or scenario methods, and supporting documentation. |

Honor an explicit intensity. Otherwise choose the smallest useful treatment. Depth and answer length are separate: a deep review can produce a short answer. Apply the relevant techniques rather than working through the whole catalogue.

## Assess the information

- Separate observations, source claims, interpretations, and assumptions where the distinction affects the conclusion. Trace decisive claims to sources or supplied artifacts; say when a source has not been inspected.
- Examine source access, date, method, context, and credibility. Follow common origins: several reports repeating one source provide one evidential lineage.
- State assumptions whose failure would materially alter the conclusion. Consider relevant base rates and alternative mechanisms, including ordinary error, coincidence, or mixed causes.
- Compare plausible explanations against the same relevant evidence. Ask which observations would be more expected under one explanation than another. Weigh supporting and conflicting evidence by its quality and diagnostic value.
- Use absent evidence only when there was a reasonable opportunity to observe it. Explain that observation condition. Treat unknowns as unknowns.
- Distinguish confidence in the evidence from an event's likelihood. Use numerical probabilities when the task and basis justify them; explain their basis. A tally of consistent or inconsistent items is not a probability estimate.
- Revisit conclusions when a critical source is corrected, withdrawn, or shown to depend on another source. Test whether the assessment survives removing its weakest decisive premise.

## Select a technique

Read only the reference needed for the current work:

- [Diagnostic methods](references/diagnostic.md): key assumptions, information quality, indicators, and competing explanations.
- [Challenge methods](references/challenge.md): Devil's Advocacy, Team A/Team B, high-impact/low-probability analysis, and What If analysis.
- [Exploratory methods](references/exploration.md): brainstorming, outside-in thinking, actor perspectives, and alternative futures.
- [Evidence and adaptation](references/evidence.md): source attribution, empirical findings, and why the procedures differ from the primer. Read when discussing effectiveness or explaining methodological choices.

Constructed perspectives are analytical alternatives. Identify them as such; agreement among simulated roles supplies no independent corroboration. Ground actor perspectives in evidence about circumstances, incentives, and constraints. Clearly label hypothetical pathways, and assess their likelihood separately from their vividness or impact.

## Present the assessment

Lead with the answer and give a concise explanation of its decisive evidence. Include material uncertainty and the observations that would change the assessment. Preserve disagreement when the evidence does not resolve it; distinguish an empirical judgment from a recommendation that depends on values or costs.

Use tables when they make comparisons easier to inspect. A supporting record may contain a source ledger, key assumptions, an evidence comparison, or scenario indicators. Produce these when requested or useful to the deliverable; the skill itself does not require creating files or monitoring tasks.

State concrete findings and conditions in plain language. Avoid forcing alternatives or elaborate apparatus onto a straightforward question. Stop when the requested assessment is adequately supported, or identify the specific missing evidence needed to progress.
